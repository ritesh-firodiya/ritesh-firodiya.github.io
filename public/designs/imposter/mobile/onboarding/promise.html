The promise

This is the product's entire positioning, and it gets a screen. Every imposter app above 1M downloads is disliked for exactly one thing, and it is not the gameplay: "$15.99 a week is insanity", "$81.99 a year", accidental charges, lifetime purchases that silently re-lock.

So we say the opposite, first, before asking for anything. Three lines, each one a direct answer to a complaint sitting in a competitor's reviews today.

Nothing may appear on this screen that undercuts it — no countdown, no strikethrough price, no "limited offer". A fake urgency device under this sentence turns the sentence into a lie, and the lie is the only thing anyone would remember.

The same block appears above the price on the paywall and in Settings, which is where a suspicious user goes looking.

v2 — the purchase lives here too, and it is deliberately the SECOND button. Somebody who has just read "three full packs are free" and wants to pay anyway should not have to go hunting for the till. But it can never be the primary action: leading with a buy button on the onboarding of a game nobody has played yet is the paywall-before-play pattern this whole screen exists to argue against. Free path first, always.

The split is named, not implied. "Three packs are free" alone reads like a teaser. Naming the three — Everyday, Food & Drink, Animals — and saying the other seven are one lifetime payment means nobody discovers the shape of the deal later and feels tricked. Competitors get one-star reviews for exactly that discovery.

"Lifetime" is stated as a word, not inferred from "once". Reviews in this category are full of people who bought a "lifetime" unlock that silently re-locked, so the claim has to be explicit enough to hold us to it.
